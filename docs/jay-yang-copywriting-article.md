What is copywriting, and why does it matter?  
When I first heard of copywriting, I thought it was the little C trademark symbol that lawyers create.  
It's not.  
Copywriting is the art of transferring information from brain A to brain B in the most effective way possible. It's persuasive writing.  
I heard of copywriting through Sam Parr, who sold his media company for tens of millions, and I thought: if I can learn how to think and communicate persuasively, what can't I do?  
Naval put it well: "If you can't code, write books and blogs, record videos and podcasts."  
Code is the backend of the internet. Copywriting is the front end.  
In an age where AI can build anything, the remaining valuable edge is the ability to create distribution. If you can shift the supply/demand curve in your favor, if you can make yourself oversubscribed with opportunity, the world is yours.  
How? All media is copywriting. A blog post, a tweet, an ad, an email, a YouTube script, a short, etc. So the better you get at copywriting (at thinking persuasively), the better your content gets across the board.  
And it's not limited to "content." Investors have to persuade LPs to give them money. Founders have to convince investors to fund them, and then convince people to join their team. Employees have to convince companies to hire them. Parents have to convince their kids to go to bed on time. Teachers have to convince students to pay attention.  
It doesn't matter what you do. You won't regret learning how to communicate more effectively.  
I said this in my first YouTube video, but a singer's instrument is their voice box. A copywriter's instrument is their mind. You become dangerous.  
How to THINK like a copywriter (the 6Ps)  
The first step to getting good at copywriting is to realize it was never about the writing.  
Copywriting is a way of thinking. It's not about the words, it's about the thinking behind those words. I can give you a bunch of templates and hacks that work and make you feel like you're getting value....  
• Identity callout hooks ("Business owners, chiropractors, YouTubers")  
• Outcome-based hooks ("If you're a business owner between $1-3m and you've hit a plateau, keep watching")  
• Promise hooks ("We'll build your entire webinar funnel for free and take a percentage of what we make you").  
...But then you're stuck repeating those templates because you don't understand WHY they work. And I'd rather teach you how to fish than give you fish.  
Before I write any piece of copy for a client, I run through a process I call the 6Ps of CopyTHINKING:  
People  
Positioning  
Promise  
Proof  
Priority  
Process  
Let's walk through each of them...  
1\. People (who is your offer for?)  
You can't answer "why should they choose us?" until you answer "who is they?"  
If you're selling a marathon training program, the messaging will look completely different depending on whether your avatar is a 45 year old dad who wants to finish his first 5k or a 28 year old runner trying to qualify for Boston.  
Step 1 is getting crystal clear on WHO your avatar is. My favorite way to do that is to build what I call an "Empathy Map." You map out their current wins, frustrations, dreams, and fears.  
My personal rule: I don't start writing until I feel like I have an overabundance of material. I want to feel like I have too much to say.  
Pro tip: Talk to customers. Our best-performing ads came from transcribing sales calls and looking at what customers were saying about why they were buying, then speaking their own words back to them in ads. I've done this twice now and it crushes.  
If you describe their problem better than they can, they'll assume you have the answer. The pain is the pitch.  
2\. Positioning (why you over anyone else?)  
Positioning is how your product is perceived in the consumer's mind. It lubricates the sales process and makes it easier for other people to talk about you.  
Seth Godin said it well: "Successful brands are built with your customers talking about you, not you talking about you."  
There's a classic story about this...  
Schlitz Beer was dead last in sales. They were competing on "PURE," the same word every other beer company used. An advertiser named Claude Hopkins visited their factory and saw expensive filters, 4,000 foot artisan water wells, and a mother yeast cell. Impressive stuff. He asked why they hadn't talked about any of this.  
The Schlitz staff shrugged. "Everybody makes beer this way," they said.  
Hopkins then built an entire advertising campaign around the amazing ways Schlitz brewed their beer (none of which was different from how anyone else did it), and it worked. They were the FIRST to tell convincing facts.  
Yes, you can use AI to write. But AI can't uncover hidden advantages and angles about your client. That's on you.  
When I research positioning, I look in 3 directions:  
Out (what are competitors doing?)  
In (what are your features and strengths?)  
And forward (what do your customers value?)  
Are you starting to see a pattern? We haven't written a single piece of copy yet and I've spent all this time talking about research.  
3\. Promise (what do they get?)  
This isn't features and deliverables. Where are you helping them get to? Who are they trying to become?  
Steve Jobs didn't say "5 gigabytes of storage." He said "1,000 songs in your pocket."  
Here's an exercise I use all the time: the "so what?" test. You keep asking "so what?" until you get to the benefit that makes someone feel something.  
If you're not clear about what core benefit you're offering people, they won't be either. And if you confuse, you lose.  
4\. Proof (why trust you?)  
Proof is evidence that backs the promise. Trust is belief with evidence. Belief without evidence is called faith.  
There are 3 types of proof:  
1\. Authority by association (Elon Musk says "XYZ")  
2\. Your own results ("I went from making $8/hr as a barista to buying my dream home because of high ticket sales")  
3\. Client results ("This is Sabrina. She went from making $50,000/year as a teacher to $50,000/month as a HTS closer")  
Pro Tip: Proof is relative. Match your proof to the avatar.  
A case study from a 50 year old Asian woman will be more persuasive to other middle-aged Asian ladies. A case study from a 21 year old kid from Florida will be more persuasive to other young people in their 20s.  
Here's an exercise I love: Imagine you're deaf and mute. You can't argue, explain, or convince anyone of anything. All you can do is point at things that exist in the room.  
You can't point at "trusted by industry leaders." You can point at the logos.  
You can't point at "proven results." You can point at a number.  
You can't point at "I'm a great copywriter." But you can point at "I wrote the emails for a launch that sold $106M in 3 days."  
5\. Priority (why act now?)  
There are only two reasons people act now: They're either moving toward something pleasurable or running away from something painful. I prefer to use pain rather than pleasure because we'll jog toward pleasure, but we'll sprint from pain.  
3 ways to highlight priority:  
1\. Scarcity (limited spots).  
2\. Urgency (need to act now, workshops get sold out, webinars happen and then they're gone, launch events with no replays).  
3\. And cost of inaction (what happens if you stay on this path?).  
The gap between where they'll be and where they want to be creates deprivation, which is the cousin to motivation.  
6\. Process (how does it work?)  
Process is an explanation for how you get results. If I said "we'll help you lose weight," that could mean calorie counting, fasting, GLP-1, or pilates. Each of those is a different process.  
A good process does 4 things:  
1\. It explains why other solutions fail  
2\. Creates a believable path  
3\. Feels different from what's already out there  
4\. And is easy to understand.  
How to find yours:  
1\. What do most people believe?  
2\. What do you believe instead?  
3\. Why is yours true?  
"Everyone thinks X. The problem is Y. So the solution is Z."  
For example: "Everyone thinks you have to start a business to make money online. But you can get paid without being an influencer or building a whole business. It's called high ticket sales."  
Or: "Everyone thinks more activity equals more progress. But you can scale faster by identifying the limiting factor and solving it. We call this the 'value acceleration method.'"

How to WRITE like a copywriter (the 5Cs)  
Once you know how to think, the next step is learning how to write. And the first lesson is that you have to throw out everything you learned in school.  
In school, you wrote five paragraph essays, used big words to sound smart, avoided first person, and had a teacher who was paid to read your writing.  
In the real world, nobody owes you their attention. You have to capture it in half a second or you lose. Assume your reader is busy, distracted, and reading at a 3rd grade level.  
I break writing down into 5Cs:  
Clear (they understand it)  
School taught you big words make you sound smart. Wrong. If the reader has to work, you lost. Clear beats clever, every time.  
The fix isn't to try harder to be clear. It's to remove what isn't clear. Cut the jargon, cut the big words, cut anything that makes the reader stop.  
Pro Tip: Uhe "there's a fire downstairs" test:  
Bad: "Hey guys, uh I want to tell you something that is of the utmost importance. Are you ready? Okay, so… uh, what was I saying? Oh yeah, so downstairs we might have a problem. There's something going on downstairs. We should all probably check it out."  
Good: "Hey, there's a fire downstairs. I need you to open the window and jump out."  
Tool: run your writing through the Hemingway App. It's free and shows you what reading level your copy is at. Aim for 6th grade or lower.  
If they go "huh?", you failed. No matter how pretty it was.  
Concise (no wasted words)  
School rewarded word counts. Pad it to 5 pages. In the world outside school, every word earns its place or gets cut.  
I use the $10 test: if every word in your copy cost you $10, what would you cut while still keeping the quality?  
A good exercise: start with tweets. The character limit makes padding impossible. Master the tweet and everything longer gets tighter.  
Shorter almost always wins.  
Concrete (they can picture it)  
Clear and concrete are not the same. Clear means they understand it. Concrete means they can picture it. You can be perfectly clear and still vague, and vague is forgettable.  
"We help businesses grow" is clear. "Took a roofing company from 12 leads a month to 47 in 60 days" is concrete.  
And when you're trying to explain something big, zoom in. "I love you" is clear. "Sometimes I wake up and I put my arm out to see if you're still there. Then I roll over and sniff the pillow and smell your shampoo and fall back asleep." That's concrete.  
Pro Tip: Uhe Name Test: if you covered your name and profile picture, would people know it was your content? If the answer is no, you're not being specific enough.  
Clear gets understood. Concrete gets remembered.  
Conversational (their language)  
School taught one stiff voice. No "I," no "you," no contractions. In the real world, you write the way a human talks. And not how you talk, how they talk.  
Match your reader's in-group language. 20 year olds and 50 year old lawyers need different words for the same idea. This is why understanding PEOPLE (the first P) is so important.  
"Please do not hesitate to reach out" becomes "text me if you need anything."  
Talk with them, not at them.  
Cadence (it reads like music)  
A run of same-length sentences drones, and the reader checks out. Cadence is the rhythm you get by varying sentence length.  
Short sentences hit hard. Medium sentences carry the thought forward. Then, once the reader is rested, you let a longer sentence build with momentum and roll toward a crescendo before you land it on something short again. That's music.  
Read it aloud and fix wherever you stumble. Or squint at the page: varied shape is good, one solid rectangle is bad.  
One guardrail here: you're not writing poetry. Good copy is like a glass window. It never draws attention to the words themselves. We're in the influence business, not the entertainment business.  
Hooks and headlines  
David Ogilvy once wrote that five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. It's not an overexaggeration to say your hook is 90% of how a post performs.  
There are a lot of frameworks for writing hooks. Here's the one that's worked best for me:  
Virality \= Benefit x Relevance x Credibility / Perceived Effort  
Benefit: why should people care? Focus on the outcome, appeal to emotion, reduce uncertainty.  
Relevance: who does this apply to? Speak to your reader, hijack trends, use their lingo.  
Credibility: why listen to you? Use numbers, borrow authority, share personal experience.  
Perceived Effort: how easy is it to read or implement? Make it simple, save them time, use social proof.  
The best hooks score high on all 4\. The worst ones nail benefit but miss relevance, or have credibility but feel like too much effort to engage with.  
Body copy  
There are a lot of ways to write the body of a post, an email, or a script. It depends on what you're writing. But there are two frameworks I come back to over and over.  
What, Why, How  
What: tell people what the idea is. "You should drink more water."  
Why: tell them why it matters. "Water improves your energy, your skin, your digestion, and your ability to focus."  
How: tell them how to do it. "Drink a full glass of water before every meal. That alone gets you at least 24 oz a day without thinking about it."  
But, Therefore  
Most people tell stories using "and then."  
I went to the grocery store and then I bought milk and then I grabbed oranges and then I paid with my credit card and then I went home and then I went to bed.  
There's no tension or surprise, nothing to pull you forward.  
The best stories replace "and" with "but" and "therefore."  
I went to the grocery store, but I forgot my wallet at home. Therefore I drove back to get it, but when I got there, someone I didn't recognize was standing in my kitchen.  
CTAs  
We're not trying to be influencers. We're trying to sign clients and grow our business.  
A good CTA has 4 parts: who, what, when, and how.  
"If you're a copywriter starting from zero and you want your first client in the next 90 days, grab the workbook. It's the first link in the description, it's free, and it takes about 20 minutes to fill in."  
One CTA to one avatar for one offer.  
How to get better at copywriting  
The fastest way to get better at writing is to read great writing and write more.  
When I first started, I immersed myself completely. I subscribed to every copywriter's email list I could find. I read old advertisements. I took courses, read books, watched YouTube videos. I listened to podcasts when I worked out, did chores, and waited in the cafeteria line at school.  
I created a swipe file of every piece of content and headline that stopped me mid-scroll, and I studied why it worked.  
I did copywork for 30 minutes every day for 6 months straight. Copywork is when you handwrite your favorite writing with pen and paper to internalize the rhythm and structure.  
Every single day, I wrote at least 10 tweets.  
And I worked at copywriting agencies to get reps and learn how an agency operates from the inside.  
You don't want money. You want skills. This is why lottery winners go broke again. YOU are the win. You don't want financial security. You want financial confidence: the confidence that even if you went back to zero, you could make it all back because of the skill set you have.  
How to make money from copywriting  
Online business comes down to traffic plus conversion plus offer. Copywriting helps with the traffic and conversion part. Here's how the funnel breaks down:  
Social media (shorts and longs) → Ads → VSL → Landing page → Emails/SMS → Offer  
You can specialize in any layer. Social media ghostwriting, email copywriting, landing page copy, or ad creative strategy. But one rule applies across all of them: go high-ticket and target people who have money.  
Wealthy clients pay more, pay faster, and are easier to work with.  
If I were starting over today, I'd pick one of two lanes:  
Lane 1: tech startups (Series A or above), specialize in LinkedIn and ads.  
Lane 2: info brands with email lists over 25k subscribers, specialize in email copywriting. Offer to write a full sequence for free, and take 10-15% of the revenue you generate from email.  
How to craft an offer  
To make more money, create more value. Value breaks down into 3 categories: save people time, make people money, or reduce risk.  
Here's an example of putting all 3 together:  
"I'll write a full email sequence for free, by myself, in your voice, in under 14 days, and generate an extra $100k for your business, or I'll work for free until you do. When I succeed, I take 10%."  
If people keep saying no, ask yourself: am I making it hard for them to say yes? (Reduce the time investment on their end.) Am I not showing how I'd make them money? (Explain it better.) Do they not believe I can do it? (Show results and proof.)  
How to get clients  
If you have no money, you should have no shame.  
When I started, I was a 17 year old kid with no degree and no decades of experience. But I had time, energy, and drive. So I created pitch decks for prospective clients and delivered them before anyone asked me to. I'd show up to sales calls with decks on how I'd grow their socials, with specific ideas to implement.  
To get an opportunity, you need to show two things: that you want it, and that you can do it. The best way to show both is to do the work upfront, before anyone asks.  
My favorite question when approaching a potential client: how can I make this a no-brainer for them? How can I reduce the friction it takes for them to say yes?  
The Pitch Deck Method  
TARGET someone 1-2 steps ahead of where you are. AUDIT where they're falling short and where you can add value. Then GIFT them a pitch deck, a Loom video, a memo, or a website mockup.  
This is how I got my first break working with Noah Kagan. I didn't send a cold email asking for a job. I showed up with a pitch deck full of specific ideas, and I made the decision to bring me on easy for him.  
I write more about this in my new book 'Work With The Best'.

I know that was a lot. And it's just the tip of the iceberg. But if you take one idea away from this, let it be this: Just start. Start messy, imperfect, and without complete information. You'll feel like you don't know enough, and you're right, you don't. Nobody does at the beginning.  
Fundamentally, you will not get poorer from learning how to persuade and communicate. Copywriting is a meta skill, and it's 100% worth learning even at a fundamental level.  
And the next time you feel discouraged, ask yourself: does giving up increase the likelihood I get what I want?
